The cityThe city gets a screen, continuedDigital arts

The most interesting part of the Biennial may not be in a gallery at all. In the coming months a large screen for digital art will go up in the new vestibule of the Glòries metro station, the entrance that leads straight into DHub, with a twin in the building’s hall.

It will be called Pantalla DHub, and from December it will carry “Resonances. The city that resonates”, a programme curated by İrem Erkin in twelve chapters, each about a different side of the city. The names are a fair map of Barcelona’s scene: Onionlab, Domestic Data Streamers, Hamill Industries, Desilence, Six N. Five, alongside Universal Everything, Sofia Crespo and Wang & Söderström.

Art that you pass on the way to somewhere else is still art. It may be the art most people see.

That is a quiet change of address. A screen in a metro hall is not a museum wall; nobody buys a ticket, and most people are on their way somewhere else. Work made for it has to be legible in ten seconds and still worth a second look on the hundredth morning.

The rest of the programme is built to bring the public in. Zachary Lieberman opened the week with a drawing workshop at DHub. From 4 to 20 December, Domestic Data Streamers and the anthropologist and filmmaker Aída Bueno show “1.5 minutes”, a piece made from colonial photographs and generative AI. On 10 December, CREATEACH spends a day on art, AI and how we decide what to make with them.

Under a +Biennial label, the city’s own players join in: Parallel Futures, Talking Galleries at Arts Santa Mònica, Anna Carreras at La Capella, and a mapping of Casa Batlló by Joanie Lemercier and Juliette Bibasse with Maria Arnal.

In February the professionals arrive: The Shift Salon with London’s Barbican on 2 and 3 February, a symposium on 3 and 4, and Circuit on the 5th, when local studios open their doors.

Why it matters to us Screens are becoming public space

Pantalla DHub is a city doing at scale what Sotabosc TV does one room at a time: putting culture on the screens people already walk past. The difference is the room. In a metro hall you have seconds; in a café, a studio or a coworking space you have half an hour, and a screen can tell a longer story about the city outside.
